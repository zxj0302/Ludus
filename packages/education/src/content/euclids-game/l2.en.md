# Euclid's Game — Winning Moves Hidden in the Golden Ratio

## 1. Play

Two piles of stones. On your turn, subtract an **integer multiple** of the smaller pile from the **larger** pile (at least 1×; you may clear the entire larger pile). After each move, reorder so the larger count comes first. Whoever makes the larger pile become 0 wins.

Choose whether you or the AI moves first. Default position [34, 21] — can you find the winning multiple?

## 2. Think

- Why is [3, 2] often too late for the player to move first?
- Why does [5, 3] let you drop the opponent into a losing position in one move?
- How is this related to the **Euclidean algorithm** you learned in school?
- Is there a one-glance formula without simulating every move?

## 3. Reveal

Each move mirrors one step of the **subtractive Euclidean algorithm**: peel off copies of the smaller number from the larger.

**Losing positions (P-positions)** use the golden ratio $\varphi = \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2} \approx 1.618$. For a normalized position $(a, b)$ with $a \ge b > 0$:

| Condition | Verdict |
|-----------|---------|
| $a = b$ | **N**: take all in one move |
| $a > b$ and $\lfloor b\varphi \rfloor \ge a$ and $a < b\varphi$ | **P**: current player loses with perfect play |
| Otherwise | **N**: a winning move exists |

**Intuition**: when the larger pile is “pinned” near $\varphi$ times the smaller, every legal move helps the opponent; otherwise you can always drop them into a P-position.

Unlike **Nim** (XOR) or **Wythoff** (Beatty sequences), the star here is **gcd / Euclidean structure + φ**.

## 4. Solve

**Example 1 — P-position [3, 2]**

```
⌊2·φ⌋ = ⌊3.236⌋ = 3
3 ≤ 3 and 3 < 3.236 → P-position
Only legal k is 1: 3−1×2 = 1 → [2,1] (N)
No move leaves a P-position → first player loses with perfect play
```

**Example 2 — N-position [13, 5]**

```
⌊5·φ⌋ = ⌊8.09⌋ = 8
8 < 13 → N-position

Legal k: 1, 2
k=2: 13 − 2×5 = 3 → [5, 3]
Check [5,3]: ⌊3·φ⌋=4, 4≥3 and 3<4.854 → P ✓

Winning move: take 2× (−10)
```

The solver tests P/N in O(1), then scans at most $\lfloor a/b \rfloor$ multiples for a winning move; the sidebar can show a full optimal sequence.

**Hand-choice hint**: N → you go first; P → let the AI go first.

## 5. Create

The generator samples $(a,b)$ by difficulty band:

- **Easy**: biased toward N-positions (first player favored)
- **Hard**: biased toward P-positions (second player favored)

A `seed` reproduces the same pair. A new level = new $(a,b)$ plus a fresh choice of who moves first.
