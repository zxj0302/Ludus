# Nim — Complete Mathematics & Algorithms

## 1. Play

Nim is the classic representative of **impartial combinatorial games**: two players, perfect information, no randomness, identical legal moves for both players. A few piles of stones, each turn a player removes any positive number of stones (possibly the entire pile) from a single pile. The player who takes the last stone wins.

## 2. Think

### 2.1 Questions

- How can we mathematically determine whether any Nim position is winning or losing?
- The winning strategy for Nim-sum = 0 — why does it always hold? What is the core insight?
- If we generalize Nim to arbitrary impartial games, is there a similar "universal solver"?
- How do we guarantee that generated puzzles are N-positions (the current player can win)?

### 2.2 Intuition

The magic of XOR lies in its ability to simultaneously capture "balance" and provide instructions for "how to break it": **XOR = 0 is like all binary bits across every pile being perfectly paired — any legal move disrupts some of those pairings; XOR ≠ 0 guarantees a countermove that zeros the XOR again.**

## 3. Reveal

### 3.1 Formal Definitions

**Definition 1 (Nim position)**: A Nim position is a finite sequence $(a_1, a_2, \dots, a_k)$ where $a_i \in \mathbb{N}$ is the stone count in pile $i$. Denote $S = \{(a_1, \dots, a_k) \mid k \ge 1, a_i \in \mathbb{N}\}$.

**Definition 2 (Legal move)**: From position $(a_1, \dots, a_k)$, a legal move chooses a pile index $i$ and a positive integer $r$ ($1 \le r \le a_i$), producing $(a_1, \dots, a_i - r, \dots, a_k)$.

**Definition 3 (P-position and N-position)**:

- **P-position (Previous-player winning)**: a position where *every* legal move leads to a winning position for the opponent.
- **N-position (Next-player winning)**: a position where there exists *at least one* legal move to a P-position.

Recursively:
- The terminal position $(0, 0, \dots, 0)$ is a **P-position** (the previous player took the last stone; the current player has no moves and thus loses).
- Position $p$ is an N-position $\iff \exists$ a legal move to some P-position.
- Position $p$ is a P-position $\iff \forall$ legal moves lead to N-positions.

### 3.2 Bouton's Theorem

> **Bouton's Theorem (1901)**: A Nim position $(a_1, \dots, a_k)$ is a **P-position** if and only if its Nim-sum is zero:
> $$a_1 \oplus a_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus a_k = 0$$
> where $\oplus$ is bitwise XOR.

#### Proof (by induction on total stones)

Induct on $N = \sum_{i=1}^k a_i$, the total number of stones.

**Base case**: $N = 0$, i.e. $(0, 0, \dots, 0)$. Nim-sum = 0, and this is a P-position (no legal moves). The base case holds.

**Induction hypothesis**: Assume Bouton's Theorem holds for all positions with total stones $< N$.

**Induction step**: Consider position $p = (a_1, \dots, a_k)$ with total stones $N > 0$.

Two directions:

**(⇒) If Nim-sum = 0, then $p$ is a P-position.**

By the induction hypothesis, we need to show: every legal move from Nim-sum = 0 leads to a position with Nim-sum ≠ 0.

Pick pile $i$ and remove $r$ stones ($1 \le r \le a_i$). The new pile size is $a_i' = a_i - r$. The new Nim-sum is:

$$s' = a_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus a_i' \oplus \cdots \oplus a_k$$

Since XOR is self-inverse ($x \oplus x = 0$):

$$s' = s \oplus a_i \oplus a_i' \quad \text{where } s = \text{original Nim-sum} = 0$$

$$s' = 0 \oplus a_i \oplus a_i' = a_i \oplus a_i'$$

Since $a_i \neq a_i'$ (because $r \ge 1$), $a_i \oplus a_i' \neq 0$. Therefore $s' \neq 0$.

Thus every legal move from Nim-sum = 0 leads to a position with Nim-sum ≠ 0. By the induction hypothesis, these are all N-positions. Hence the current position is a P-position.

**(⇐) If Nim-sum ≠ 0, then $p$ is an N-position.**

Let $s = a_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus a_k \neq 0$. We need to construct a legal move to a position with Nim-sum = 0.

Let $d$ be the highest set bit of $s$. Since $s \neq 0$, $d$ exists and $2^d \le s < 2^{d+1}$.

Since bit $d$ of $s$ is 1 and $s$ is the XOR of all $a_i$, at least one $a_i$ must also have bit $d$ set. Pick such an $a_i$.

Let $a_i' = a_i \oplus s$. Since $a_i$ has bit $d$ set and $s$ has bit $d$ set, the XOR clears that bit, so $a_i' < a_i$ (the highest differing bit changed from 1 to 0, decreasing the value). Also $a_i' \ge 0$.

The removal amount $r = a_i - a_i'$ satisfies $1 \le r \le a_i$. After removing $r$ stones from pile $i$, the new Nim-sum is:

$$s' = s \oplus a_i \oplus a_i' = s \oplus a_i \oplus (a_i \oplus s) = s \oplus a_i \oplus a_i \oplus s = 0$$

We have successfully constructed a legal move to a Nim-sum = 0 position. By the induction hypothesis, this successor is a P-position. Hence the current position is an N-position.

**QED.**

> **Corollary**: Bouton's Theorem yields an $O(k)$ time, $O(1)$ space algorithm for determining win/loss ($k$ = number of piles), and a linear-time construction of the winning strategy.

### 3.3 Algebraic Properties of XOR

The XOR operation underlying the Nim-sum has these key properties:

| Property | Formula | Meaning |
|----------|---------|---------|
| **Commutative** | $a \oplus b = b \oplus a$ | Order doesn't matter |
| **Associative** | $(a \oplus b) \oplus c = a \oplus (b \oplus c)$ | Can group arbitrarily |
| **Identity** | $a \oplus 0 = a$ | 0 is the identity element |
| **Self-inverse** | $a \oplus a = 0$ | Every element is its own inverse |
| **Cancellation** | $a \oplus b = a \oplus c \Rightarrow b = c$ | XOR $a$ on both sides |

Self-inverse is the heart of Nim: a P-position (XOR = 0) when disturbed becomes non-zero, and a non-zero position can always be zeroed by a single XOR operation — **self-inverse = the "perfect balance - break - restore" triad**.

### 3.4 The Sprague-Grundy Theorem

Bouton's Theorem solves Nim. The Sprague-Grundy Theorem (1935-1939) generalizes this insight to **all impartial games**:

> **Theorem (Sprague-Grundy)**: Every position in every impartial game is equivalent to a Nim heap of size equal to the position's Grundy number $g$. The disjunctive sum of multiple impartial games equals the XOR of their Grundy numbers. A position is a P-position iff its Grundy number is 0.

#### Recursive Definition of Grundy Number

$$g(p) = \operatorname{mex}\{g(q) \mid q \text{ is a successor of } p\}$$

where $\operatorname{mex}$ (minimum excludant) is the smallest non-negative integer not present in the set.

For Nim itself, a single heap of size $n$ has Grundy number exactly $n$ (successors are $0, 1, \dots, n-1$, whose $\operatorname{mex} = n$). Therefore multiple Nim heaps = XOR of their Grundy numbers, matching Bouton's Theorem exactly.

**Significance**: Sprague-Grundy reduces any impartial game to Nim — solving any impartial game reduces to computing Grundy numbers. This is a landmark result in combinatorial game theory.

### 3.5 Complexity Classification

- **Complexity Class**: **P** (polynomial time)
- **Key Justification**:
  - P/N-position determination: $O(k)$ — a single XOR pass
  - Winning move construction: $O(k)$ — find the pile with the highest set bit of $s$
  - Space complexity: $O(1)$ — only need to track current Nim-sum and best move index
- **Contrast**: Nim's extremely simple mathematical structure (XOR) gives it the most efficient algorithm among all impartial games. Computing Grundy numbers for general impartial games can be exponential.

## 4. Solve

### 4.1 Nim-sum Solver

The Nim solver is minimal — one XOR pass and one move construction:

```typescript
function computeNimSum(piles: number[]): number {
  return piles.reduce((a, b) => a ^ b, 0);
}

function isPPosition(piles: number[]): boolean {
  return computeNimSum(piles) === 0;
}

function findWinningMove(piles: number[]): NimMove | null {
  const nimSum = computeNimSum(piles);
  if (nimSum === 0) return null;          // P-position → no winning move
  let bestMove = null, bestRemaining = Infinity;
  for (let i = 0; i < piles.length; i++) {
    const target = piles[i]! ^ nimSum;    // a_i' = a_i ⊕ s
    if (target < piles[i]!) {
      const remaining = piles.reduce((a, b) => a + b, 0) - (piles[i]! - target);
      if (remaining < bestRemaining) {    // pick move with fewest remaining stones
        bestRemaining = remaining;
        bestMove = { pileIndex: i, removeCount: piles[i]! - target };
      }
    }
  }
  return bestMove;
}
```

#### Workflow

1. Compute XOR of all piles → Nim-sum $s$
2. If $s = 0$ → no winning move (P-position)
3. If $s \neq 0$ → iterate piles to find one with `piles[i] ⊕ s < piles[i]`
4. Remove `piles[i] - (piles[i] ⊕ s)` stones from that pile

#### Complexity Analysis

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Time Complexity | $O(k)$, $k$ = number of piles |
| Space Complexity | $O(1)$ |
| Win/loss determination | One XOR scan, $O(k)$ |
| Winning move construction | $O(k)$ (worst case traverse all piles) |
| Solver output | One winning move (if exists) or empty (if losing) |

## 5. Create

> **Assumption**: The analysis and strategies in this chapter assume the player chooses to go **first**. If the player chooses to go second, the AI moves first which reverses the position type, and the player's strategy adjusts accordingly.
>
> Unlike §3's formal definition, §5 addresses the core question: **how do we guarantee that generated Nim puzzles are well-formed and have controllable difficulty?**

### 5.1 Guarantee I: Solvability — the player chooses

#### Problem

The generated initial position should allow the player to obtain a favorable position by choosing whether to go first or second.

#### Implementation

**Random generation (current default strategy)**:

1. Determine pile count $k$ and max stones $\text{maxStone}$ from the difficulty tier
2. Randomly generate each pile in $[1, \text{maxStone}]$
3. Positions are naturally distributed — they may be P-positions (Nim-sum = 0) or N-positions (Nim-sum ≠ 0)

#### Strategy

- **N-position** (Nim-sum ≠ 0): choose to go first, a winning move exists
- **P-position** (Nim-sum = 0): choose to go second, let the AI break the balance, then the player gets a winning move

The player sees the full layout before deciding. The Hint/Solve buttons recommend the optimal choice based on the Nim-sum.

### 5.2 Guarantee II: Unique Solution

#### Problem

Does an N-position have exactly one winning move, or more than one?

#### Criterion

An N-position may have multiple winning moves. For example, position [3, 4, 5] (Nim-sum = 2):

```
Pile 3: 3 ⊕ 2 = 1 < 3 → take 2 ✓
Pile 4: 4 ⊕ 2 = 6 ≥ 4 ✗
Pile 5: 5 ⊕ 2 = 7 ≥ 5 ✗
→ exactly one winning move
```

Position [2, 2, 1] (Nim-sum = 1):

```
Pile 2: 2 ⊕ 1 = 3 ≥ 2 ✗
Pile 2: 2 ⊕ 1 = 3 ≥ 2 ✗
Pile 1: 1 ⊕ 1 = 0 < 1 → take 1 ✓
→ still only one winning move
```

**General case**: The number of winning moves equals the count of piles $i$ satisfying $a_i \oplus s < a_i$, where $s = \text{Nim-sum}$. There is always at least one (since $s$'s highest set bit appears in at least one pile), but there may be more.

Nim level generation does not require a unique solution — the player merely needs to find **any** winning move to win.

### 5.3 Guarantee III: Known Optimal Solution

#### Problem

What does "optimal solution" mean for Nim?

#### Definition

Nim's optimal goal is not "minimum number of moves" (the game can end in 1 move), but **to most straightforwardly guide the opponent into a P-position**. Bouton's Theorem's constructive method naturally yields one winning move — no search or optimization needed.

- **Single winning move**: when exactly one pile satisfies $a_i \oplus s < a_i$, that move is optimal.
- **Multiple winning moves**: any move that zeros the Nim-sum is equally valid. The solver picks the move that leaves the **fewest total remaining stones** (ending the game fastest, leaving the simplest remaining state).
- **optimalSteps = 1**: The Nim solver outputs **one move** — the step from the current N-position to a P-position. After the player takes this move, the game enters a P-position, and the opponent is theoretically in a losing position.

> **Note**: "Fewest remaining stones" does NOT guarantee the minimum number of plies to win. The opponent, when in a P-position, may employ delaying tactics (e.g., taking only 1 stone per turn), stretching the game. To strictly minimize moves, one would need **minimax search** or **retrograde analysis** over the full game tree — computing the winning depth for each state backwards from terminal positions. This is $O(\text{state space})$, which for Nim grows exponentially with pile count and stone sizes, not polynomial. The current heuristic is a good practical approximation and guarantees the core objective: reaching a P-position in one move.

#### How Generation Guarantees It

The P-distance walk naturally guarantees: from the generated N-position, at least one move back to a P-position exists. The solver finds one in $O(k)$ time and outputs `moves.length = 1`.

### 5.4 Guarantee IV: Difficulty Control

#### Problem

How do we quantify Nim position difficulty and precisely control it during generation?

#### Difficulty Factor Framework

| Factor | Symbol | Quantification | Effect on Difficulty | Controllability |
|--------|--------|---------------|---------------------|-----------------|
| Pile count | $k$ | Total number of piles | Larger $k$ → more complex XOR mental arithmetic. $k=2$ simplifies to an equality check (equal piles = P-position); $k\ge 3$ requires full binary computation | **Continuously tunable** (2-6 piles) |
| Max stones | $\max(a_i)$ | Maximum stones in any pile | Larger numbers → more binary bits → harder XOR calculation. Small numbers (1-7) need 3 bits; larger numbers (8-15) need 4 bits | **Continuously tunable** |
| Number distribution | — | Distribution pattern of stone counts | Uniform vs skewed: skewed distributions make bit alignment less obvious → harder mental XOR | **Indirectly tunable** |
| Winning move conspicuity | — | Difference between $a_i \oplus s$ and $a_i$ | Moderate removal ratios feel "reasonable"; very small (take 1) or very large (almost whole pile) removals are easier to spot | **Indirectly tunable** |

#### Difficulty Tier Mapping

| Tier | Pile Count | Max Stones | Binary Bits | Mental Difficulty |
|------|-----------|------------|-------------|-------------------|
| 🟢 Easy | 2-3 | 3-5 | 2-3 | Simple: 2 piles just check equality; 3 piles need basic XOR |
| 🟡 Medium | 4-5 | 4-8 | 3 | Medium: 4-5 piles require full binary alignment |
| 🔴 Hard | 6 | 5-10 | 3-4 | Hard: 6 piles split attention, high-bit XOR mental load |

#### How the Generator Implements Difficulty Control

```
function generateLevel(targetDifficulty):
    1. k = pickFromPreset(targetDifficulty).pileCount
    2. maxS = pickFromPreset(targetDifficulty).maxStone
    3. (a_1, ..., a_k) = generatePPosition(k, maxS)
    4. walk 1 step to N-position
    5. return { piles, difficulty: computeScore(k, maxS) }
```

#### Difficulty Score Formula

$$\text{pileFactor} = \frac{k - 2}{4} \quad (2 \to 0,\; 6 \to 1)$$
$$\text{stoneFactor} = \frac{\max(a_i) - 3}{7} \quad (3 \to 0,\; 10 \to 1)$$
$$\text{score} = (0.5 \cdot \text{pileFactor} + 0.5 \cdot \text{stoneFactor}) \times 100$$

The score is clamped to [0, 100]. As pile count increases from 2 to 6 and max stones from 3 to 10, the difficulty score smoothly transitions from minimum to maximum.

#### Difficulty Verification Loop

Every generated puzzle must pass the following checks before publication:

1. **N-position verification**: Nim-sum ≠ 0 (current player has a winning strategy).
2. **Nim-sum correctly computed**: XOR of all piles matches the solver result.
3. **At least one winning move exists**: the solver returns a valid move.
4. **The move zeros the Nim-sum**: after applying the move, the new Nim-sum = 0.
5. **Difficulty score is correct**: pile count and max stones fall within the target tier range.
