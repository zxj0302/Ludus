## 1. 玩 · Play

Peg Solitaire has a one-line rule: choose a peg, jump over an adjacent peg into the empty hole beyond it, and remove the peg you jumped over. Every move removes exactly one peg, so the board does not drift; it tightens.

**Hook 1: the last three moves are often not a cleanup, but a trial.** A board with only five pegs can look spacious and still have no legal jump. A crowded little cross can still contain a beautiful chain. Peg Solitaire lies politely: empty space is not the same thing as freedom.

This Ludus version uses the English 33-hole cross board. Difficulty changes the target, not just the number of pegs:

- Easy: leave at most 3 pegs.
- Medium: leave exactly 1 peg anywhere.
- Hard: leave exactly 1 peg in the center.

**Rigorous claim 1: every legal move reduces the peg count by exactly 1.** A jump removes the `from` peg and the `over` peg, then fills the `to` hole, so the net change is \(-1\). From the classic center-empty start with 32 pegs, any one-peg finish must therefore use exactly 31 moves. That is a counting invariant, not a hunch.

Mini challenge: before pressing Solve, look at landing holes instead of starting pegs. Will this move create future three-hole jump patterns, or will it strand a lonely peg?

## 2. 思考挑战 · Think

Peg Solitaire is difficult because the resource you spend is also the bridge you need later. You want to remove pegs, but each removal can also destroy future mobility. A sparse board can behave like a city after too many bridges have been demolished.

**Hook 2: the dangerous habit is “jump into every hole you see.”** Greedy jumps often leave isolated pegs. They look harmless, but isolated pegs are the seeds of dead ends: they cannot jump, and they cannot be jumped over.

Ask three questions while playing:

- After this move, do I create new straight triples?
- Am I pushing material away from the center, making the Hard target harder?
- Am I breaking symmetry that could have kept two sides of the puzzle cooperating?

**Rigorous claim 2: legal moves come only from fixed orthogonal triples.** The English board has no diagonal jumps and no two-hole leaps. The solver precomputes every `from-over-to` triple, then checks whether `from` and `over` contain pegs and `to` is empty. A diagonal that “looks close” is still illegal.

Mini challenge: if a position has no legal jumps and 4 pegs, does Easy count it as solved? No: Easy requires \(\le 3\). With 4 pegs it is a dead end; with 3 pegs it has already met the relaxed target.

## 3. 揭示抽象 · Reveal

Number the 33 holes from 0 to 32. Each hole is either occupied or empty, so a position is a subset of those holes. In code, it is a bitmask: bit \(i\) is 1 when hole \(i\) contains a peg.

**Hook 3: this is not just a board; it is a 33-bit password.** Humans see a cross-shaped puzzle. The solver sees one number. A jump flips three bits: `from=1` becomes 0, `over=1` becomes 0, and `to=0` becomes 1.

Formally:

- State: \(S \subseteq \{0,1,\dots,32\}\).
- Move: \((a,b,c)\), three adjacent holes in a straight line.
- Legal condition: \(a,b\in S\) and \(c\notin S\).
- Transition: \(S'=(S-\{a,b\})\cup\{c\}\).

**Rigorous claim 3: the naive state-space upper bound is \(2^{33}=8,589,934,592\).** This counts all occupied/empty patterns, not only positions reachable from the classic start. The reachable set is far smaller, but the upper bound explains why blind enumeration of every bitmask is not a good story.

Symmetry is the first cheat code. The English cross board can be rotated and reflected. A board rotated 90 degrees may look different to you, but for the solver it can be the same problem. We store a canonical representative so all mirrored versions share one visited key.

## 4. 求解器演示 · Solver

The solver behaves like an explorer. It tries legal jumps, records positions it has already seen, and avoids revisiting equivalent states. Small endgames use BFS to find shortest paths; deeper positions use DFS within a time budget to find a reachable target. Both use the same D4 symmetry canonical key.

**Hook 4: the solver is not an oracle; it is a detective with a map.** It does not declare a position solved because it “feels promising.” It must actually reach the target. If the time budget runs out, it reports timeout honestly instead of pretending that “not found” means “impossible.”

The visualization reports:

- Nodes explored: how many distinct positions the detective inspected.
- Solution length: how many jumps the current route uses.
- Max depth: how far ahead the search reached.
- Search mode: `bfs` or `dfs`.
- Symmetry: `D4`, meaning rotations and reflections are merged.

**Rigorous claim 4: BFS gives a shortest path when all moves have unit cost.** Peg Solitaire jumps all cost one step, so when BFS first reaches the target in a small endgame, that path length is shortest. DFS is more memory-friendly and useful for deep positions, but it does not automatically prove shortestness.

Play tip: ask for a hint first, then continue by yourself. If you get stuck, use Solve to inspect the whole route. Treat the solver like a coach, not a remote control.

## 5. 出题 · Create

Creating a puzzle is not the same as deleting random pegs. Random deletion often creates boards that look like puzzles but have no route to the goal. This generator chooses a target from the difficulty range, samples curated endgames, and verifies reachability with the solver.

**Hook 5: a good puzzle is a magic trick; a bad puzzle is just a broken lock.** The magic trick surprises you but can be reconstructed. The broken lock simply refuses to open. The generator's baseline promise is solvability; uniqueness and unique-shortest certificates are separate, harder promises.

The current strategy is intentionally honest:

- Use `difficultyRange` to choose `le3`, `one-any`, or `one-center`.
- Prefer curated puzzles, with light dig-out variation when safe.
- Run the solver to verify that the selected target is reachable.
- Set `uniqueSolution: false`, because v1 does not prove uniqueness or unique shortestness.

**Rigorous claim 5: this version does not turn “found one route” into “there is only one route.”** `optimalSteps` comes from the solver or known puzzle metadata, while `uniqueSolution` remains false. The generator guarantees a reachable target; it does not claim a unique solution.

Final challenge: generate a Hard board and predict whether the final peg can be pulled back to the center. That is the charm of Peg Solitaire: you are not merely reducing the count; you are choreographing the final landing place.
